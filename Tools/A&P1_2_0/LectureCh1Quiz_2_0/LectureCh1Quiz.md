# Q: The principle of complementarity of structure and function states that:

> Anatomical information provides clues about functions, and physiological processes can be explained only in terms of underlying anatomy. Form and function are inseparable.

# Q: Which of the following scenarios best describes "autoregulation" in homeostasis?
? Think about the prefix "auto-", meaning self, without external help.
- [x] When oxygen levels drop in a tissue, local cells release chemicals that dilate nearby blood vessels.
  ^ Correct. Autoregulation is localized and doesn't require nervous or endocrine system input.
- [ ] The nervous system sends electrical signals to contract a skeletal muscle to pull a hand away from a stove.
  ^ Incorrect. This involves the nervous system, making it an example of extrinsic regulation.
- [ ] The endocrine system releases insulin into the blood after a large meal.
  ^ Incorrect. Hormonal control is a form of extrinsic regulation.
- [ ] A severe cut triggers a cascading blood clot that completely seals a vessel.
  ^ Incorrect. While local, this describes a positive feedback loop rather than simple autoregulation of a local environment.
> Autoregulation (intrinsic regulation) occurs when a cell, tissue, or organ adjusts its own activities automatically in response to an environmental change, independent of neural or endocrine signals.

# Q: In a homeostatic regulatory mechanism, what is the specific role of the control center?

  ^ Incorrect. That is the exact function of the receptor.
- [x] It receives and processes information from the receptor and sends out commands.
  ^ Correct. The control center establishes the set point and decides how to respond.
- [ ] It responds physically to the commands to either oppose or enhance the stimulus.

> The control center (often the brain or a specific gland) processes sensory input, compares it to the desired set point, and sends instructions to effectors to restore balance.

# Q: How does negative feedback help maintain homeostasis in the body?
? Think about a thermostat controlling a room's temperature.
- [ ] It amplifies the original stimulus to speed up a necessary, time-sensitive process.
  ^ Incorrect. This describes the mechanism of positive feedback.
- [x] It produces a response that opposes or negates the original stimulus.
  ^ Correct. By opposing the change, negative feedback brings conditions back to the normal set point.
- [ ] It permanently changes the set point so the body doesn't have to react to that stimulus again.
  ^ Incorrect. Set points can shift, but negative feedback itself doesn't permanently freeze them.
- [ ] It ignores minor daily variations and only activates during life-threatening events.
  ^ Incorrect. Negative feedback is constantly working behind the scenes to correct even minor variations.
> Negative feedback minimizes change. If a variable rises above normal, negative feedback lowers it; if it drops below normal, negative feedback raises it.

# Q: Which of the following is a classic example of negative feedback in the human body?

# Q: In positive feedback, the initial stimulus produces a response that:

  ^ Incorrect. Reversing the change is the exact definition of negative feedback.
- [ ] Shuts down the control center to preserve cellular energy.
  ^ Incorrect. The control center remains highly active in positive feedback.

  ^ Correct. Positive feedback creates an escalating cycle that pushes the body further from the starting point.
- [ ] Maintains a dynamic equilibrium tightly around a strict set point.
  ^ Incorrect. Positive feedback pushes the body away from homeostasis temporarily to resolve a crisis.
> Positive feedback loops escalate a process. They are typically used for dangerous or stressful events that must be completed quickly (like blood clotting or labor), rather than for long-term daily maintenance.

# Q: Why is positive feedback suitable for blood clotting but NOT for regulating body temperature?
